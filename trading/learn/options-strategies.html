01 / Options

Start with legs and cash flows

A strategy name hides a set of contracts. Write every leg: buy or sell, call or put, strike, expiry, quantity and premium. Then sum each leg’s result at the same assumed settlement price. The expiry picture does not describe every pre-expiry mark-to-market path.

  • Debit: premium paid to establish the structure.
  • Credit: premium received; this is not immediately earned profit.
  • Equal quantities and same expiry are crucial assumptions in a simple vertical-spread diagram.
02 / Options

Directional vertical spreads

A bull call spread buys a lower-strike call and sells a higher-strike call. In a simplified same-expiry model its maximum loss is the net debit and maximum gain is strike width minus debit. A bear put spread uses a higher-strike long put and a lower-strike short put. Credit spreads express the complementary payoff through short and protective long legs.

  • A bull put spread combines a short higher-strike put and a long lower-strike put.
  • A bear call spread combines a short lower-strike call and a long higher-strike call.
  • The advertised risk limit assumes the complete structure stays in place and settlement assumptions hold. Legging in or out can expose different risk.
03 / Options

Movement and range structures

A long straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike; enough movement is needed to overcome the combined premium at expiry. A long strangle uses separate out-of-the-money strikes. An iron condor combines a put credit spread below and a call credit spread above. It caps both favorable and unfavorable expiry outcomes in the simplified model.

  • A short straddle or strangle without protective wings can create very large or unlimited losses.
  • Butterflies concentrate a payoff around a region; small-looking debit does not imply a high chance of profit.
  • Calendar and diagonal spreads span expiries, so a single one-expiry intrinsic-value chart is insufficient.
  • Ratio spreads can leave excess uncovered options. Check the remaining tail exposure.
04 / Options

Hedging is a trade-off, not free protection

A protective put adds a purchased put to an underlying holding; insurance costs premium. A covered call caps upside while retaining substantial underlying downside. A collar combines a holding, a protective put and a short call, exchanging some upside for protection under stated terms.

  • Payoff labs here use hypothetical, European-style cash-settled, one-unit examples at expiry before costs.
  • Real outcomes depend on quantity, liquidity, exercise style, settlement, margin and broker handling.
  • No structure shown is a personal recommendation or an instruction to place a trade.
